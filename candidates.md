# Candidates — Absurd Micro-Startup Pitch-Off

Competition: `u-ricoxyz-absurd-micro-startup-pitch-off-12e33571`
Judge brief criteria, most important first:
1. Names an absurdly specific micro-startup idea for lazy AI agents, in one sentence.
2. Absurdly specific — narrow, oddly particular niche / customer / moment, not a general category with a joke attached.
3. Makes business sense — plausible buyer, plausible way it earns money, reason it could exist.
4. Fits lazy AI agents — work agents can run with little effort / oversight, and the sentence makes that clear.
5. Crisp and funny — absurdity + business case both landing in one read.

Selection method: MANUAL ranking against the brief criteria in their stated order,
plus pairwise checks against strong rival shapes. No Gemini judge, no NVIDIA judge,
no judge-API/proxy tournament (Rev's standing rule, 2026-11-24).

## The 15 candidates (all one sentence, all under 200 chars)

- **C1** (133 chars) — A lazy AI agent that sits on hold with your airline, rebooks the cheaper flight when prices drop, and keeps 15% of what it saves you.
- **C2** (145 chars) — A lazy AI agent that joins your daily standup, says 'no blockers,' and logs off, sold to remote workers who pay $2 a meeting to never attend one.
- **C3** (156 chars) — A service where idle agents rename, tag, and file every screenshot you take, turning your camera roll from junk drawer to searchable archive for $3 a month.
- **C4** (150 chars) — An agent that reads your calendar every Friday at 4pm and fills in your timesheet, so contractors bill every hour they actually worked, for $1 a week.
- **C5** (150 chars) — A micro-startup that lets lazy agents haggle your parking tickets down and split the fine reduction 50/50 with drivers who never read the small print.
- **C6** (160 chars) — An agent that watches your smart meter and switches your energy tariff the second a cheaper one appears, splitting the savings with households that never check.
- **C7** (145 chars) — A service where agents summarize your 47 unread Slack channels into one sentence each morning, sold to managers drowning in pings for $4 a month.
- **C8** (146 chars) — An agent that proofreads your landlord's rent-increase letters and drafts a polite counter-offer, charging tenants $5 every time the rent goes up.
- **C9** (146 chars) — A lazy agent that waits in your doctor's phone queue, books the 8am slot the moment it opens, and charges busy parents $3 per appointment secured.
- **C10** (145 chars) — An agent that scans your fridge camera, orders the milk before you run out, and takes a cut from the grocery store for every restock it triggers.
- **C11** (134 chars) — A service where agents watch your toddler's baby monitor all night and text you only when the crying sounds expensive, for $2 a night.
- **C12** (138 chars) — An agent that files your expense receipts from blurry photos and charges your accountant 10 cents per receipt it rescues from the shoebox.
- **C13** (143 chars) — A micro-startup that has lazy agents rewrite your LinkedIn headline every Monday so recruiters keep thinking you're employable, for $5 a month.
- **C14** (143 chars) — An agent that checks your flight's gate every five minutes and texts you only when it actually changes, sold to anxious flyers for $1 per trip.
- **C15** (156 chars) — A lazy agent that reads your 200 unread emails, replies to the three that matter, and archives the rest, for executives who pay $6 a week to feel caught up.

## Manual ranking (criteria in brief order)

1. **C2 — WINNER.** The moment is oddly particular (daily standup, a two-word job), the buyer is exact (remote workers, $2 a meeting), the revenue is recurring, and the laziness IS the product: join, say "no blockers", log off. Funniest line in the set, and the business case lands in the same read.
2. **C1.** Strong model (15% of savings) and the agent's work is literally waiting on hold — peak lazy. Loses to C2 on (2): flight rebooking is a known service category, while the standup ritual is a more particular moment.
3. **C9.** Very particular moment (doctor's 8am slot, phone queue), real mechanism, clear buyer (busy parents, $3 per appointment). Slightly less funny than C2/C1, and a one-off purchase beats less well than C2's daily recurring pain.
4. **C5.** Specific customer (drivers who never read the small print) and a strong 50/50 split. Loses on (3): parking fines are usually fixed amounts, so "haggle it down" is a shakier mechanism than rebooking a flight or joining a call.
5. **C4.** Very particular moment (Friday 4pm timesheet) and clear ROI (contractors bill more hours). Loses on (2)/(5): timesheet-filling is a known automation category, and the line is dry rather than funny.
6. **C6.** Good savings-split model, passive monitoring fits lazy agents. Loses on (2): "households that never check" is a broad customer, and tariff-switching is a known category.
7. **C8.** Specific trigger (rent goes up) and clear buyer (tenants, $5). Loses on (4): drafting a counter-offer is real work, not lazy work, and the niche is legal-help-shaped, not absurd-shaped.
8. **C14.** Passive monitoring fits lazy agents perfectly. Loses on (3): the payoff (a text when the gate changes) is thin, so buyer willingness at $1/trip is shakier than C1/C9.
9. **C3.** Specific chore, idle agents fit well. Loses on (2)/(3): "camera-roll junk drawer" is a familiar trope, and $3/month for screenshot filing is a weaker willingness-to-pay than $2/meeting to skip standup.
10. **C13.** Specific cadence (every Monday). Loses on (3): who pays $5/month for a headline rewrite? The value is vanity, not money saved or earned.
11. **C7.** "Managers drowning in pings" is a general category; summarisation is a generic AI use case with a number attached, not an oddly particular niche. Loses on (2).
12. **C15.** Generic email-triage category; the buyer pays "to feel caught up" — a feeling, not a result. Loses on (2)/(3).
13. **C10.** Revenue path murky: which side pays the cut, the store or the user? Buyer unclear. Loses on (3).
14. **C12.** Specific chore, but the buyer is wrong (accountants don't pay per receipt; clients do) and receipt-scanning is a known category. Loses on (2)/(3).
15. **C11.** Second-funniest line ("crying sounds expensive") but the weakest business sense: no parent pays per night for a monitor that texts them, and "expensive crying" has no plausible mechanism. The brief says it plainly: merely silly with no buyer in sight loses to silly and sellable.

## Pairwise checks vs strong rival shapes

- **vs generic category + joke** (e.g. "an AI that writes apology emails"): C2 wins on (2) — it names a moment (the standup ritual), not a category.
- **vs flight-price watcher** (C1-like): C2 wins on (2) (standup ritual is more particular than flight prices) and (4) (a two-word job is lazier than monitoring prices).
- **vs DMV line-waiter** (specific moment, clear buyer): C2 wins on (3) — skipping a daily meeting is recurring revenue; a DMV visit is a one-off.
- **vs inbox-zero agent**: C2 wins on (2) — inbox triage is a generic category; the standup is an oddly particular moment.
- **C2 vs C1 head-to-head:** C1's model (15% of savings) is arguably stronger than $2/meeting, but criteria are weighed in order and (2) comes before (3): C2's moment is more particular, and on (4) C2's agent does less — its entire job is two words. C2 wins.
- **C2 vs C9 head-to-head:** both name a queue/ritual moment; C2's buyer pain recurs daily, C9's per appointment, and C2 is funnier on (5). C2 wins.
- **C2 vs C5 head-to-head:** C5's split is strong, but its mechanism (haggling a fixed fine) is less believable than C2's (joining a call and speaking). C2 wins on (3).

## Winner

**C2** (145 characters, one sentence):

> A lazy AI agent that joins your daily standup, says 'no blockers,' and logs off, sold to remote workers who pay $2 a meeting to never attend one.
